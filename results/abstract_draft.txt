With n attempts on each of m problems, the success counts identify pass@k for k > n only through the first n moments of the success-rate distribution. Two populations with the same counts can differ in pass@k by at most 2E_n(x^k), the error of the best degree-n approximation to x^k, which stays below a tolerance eps until k is of order n^2/ln(4/eps) and exceeds 0.33 from k = 8n^2 on. This quadratic horizon is what infinitely many problems could reveal. How far benchmarks are from it can be bounded for every estimator. On 10 real count laws (Llama-3, Gemma, Pythia and Qwen models on GSM8K, MATH and CodeContests), Le Cam's two-point method with a Hellinger constraint shows that with m = 1,319 problems (the size of the GSM8K test set) and n = 32 attempts each, every estimator of pass@16n errs by at least 0.084 to 0.205, with probability at least 1/4 under some population near the observed one, for 7 of the 10 count laws (0.095 to 0.225 at m = 500); at pass@64n the floor reaches 0.31, while at pass@4n it is at most 0.044 for every count law, so there the bound leaves open whether benchmarks of the usual size suffice. The floor is close to what a standard procedure achieves at that m: on 100 benchmarks of 1,319 problems drawn from each observed distribution, a profile likelihood-ratio interval at the same probability level has a median half-width of 1.02 to 1.22 times the floor at pass@16n (0.089 to 0.222 for the count laws whose floor exceeds 0.05) and holds the true value in 97% of the draws against a nominal 75%. Over 20 resamples of the problems, the floor at pass@16n varies by a factor of at most 2.2 between the 10th and 90th percentiles (1.6 where it exceeds 0.05) and stays above 0.05 in every resample for 2 of the 3 count laws tested, whereas the number of problems needed for +-0.05, read from the same bound where a nearly flat curve crosses a threshold, moves by up to a factor of 1,200. As a sample size the bound is an order-of-magnitude statement: for pass@16n, 7 of the count laws need more than 65,000 problems for +-0.05. Attempts per problem matter more than the number of problems: at k = 512 the median requirement falls from 1.3e5 problems with n = 32 to 770 with n = 64. Counted in problems rather than in error, the same interval narrows to width 0.1 with 3.3 to 5.4 times as many problems as the bound requires in its best case (10 to 16 times for a 95% interval; pass@4n to pass@16n), while a DKW band needs 490 to 28,000 times as many. On 100 benchmarks of that size drawn from each observed distribution (22 count-law and k combinations), the interval's median width was 0.082 to 0.097, it held the true pass@k in 73% to 100% of the draws against a nominal 75%, and its midpoint was within +-0.05 of it in at least 81% of the draws of every combination. At the benchmark sizes in use (m <= 1,319) the binding limits are finite-sample. For linear estimators of the count histogram with range-based certificates, which include the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021) and the doubling estimator of Theorem 1(B), a certified error of 0.1 is reached only up to k = 1.25n-2.5n; the worst-case two-point lower bound reaches 0.1 only at k = 11.2n-34n, and between the two it is open, for worst-case populations, whether any estimator can certify that error at these m. Beyond 2n the counts give bands rather than points: in 9 of the 9 Monkey Business configurations the 95% band from 32 attempts held the held-out truth in at least 7 of 8 blocks at every k where it was computed, up to k = 8,192, and on our own streams its median width at pass@4n is 0.23. Inside a band the forecast depends on the parametric family. With ground truth up to k = 8,192 on the Monkey Business release (Brown et al., 2024), in the 6 settings where the family mattered, the family that was most accurate at k = 4n stayed within a factor of two of the best at the largest k in 6 of them, and choosing the family by its fit inside the sampled range came within a factor of two of the best family in 4. In relative terms (an exploratory analysis added after these results), forecasts on GSM8K whose pass@k errors were below 0.01 were off in the failure rate by factors of 2 to 142.
